# AI Use-Case Prioritization Framework

## Purpose

A practical decision-making framework for business leaders evaluating and comparing multiple AI opportunities.

The purpose is to help leadership focus investment, resources and attention on AI opportunities that combine meaningful business value, strategic relevance, practical feasibility, reasonable time to value and manageable risk.

AI opportunities should be prioritized as **business investments**, rather than simply ranked by technical attractiveness.

---

# 1. From Assessment to Prioritization

The **AI Opportunity Assessment Framework** evaluates an individual opportunity.

The **AI Use-Case Prioritization Framework** compares multiple assessed opportunities and determines where leadership attention and resources should be focused.

The decision journey is:

**Business Problem → Opportunity Assessment → Prioritization → Business Case → Investment Decision → Execution → Value Realization**

Prioritization is an important decision point, but it is **not the same as the final investment decision**.

A high-priority opportunity should still be supported by an appropriate business case before significant investment is committed.

---

# 2. Evaluation Dimensions

Each AI opportunity is evaluated across five dimensions.

| Dimension           |   Weight |
| ------------------- | -------: |
| Business Value      |  **40%** |
| Strategic Alignment |  **15%** |
| Feasibility         |  **15%** |
| Time to Value       |  **15%** |
| Risk Manageability  |  **15%** |
| **Total**           | **100%** |

The weighting deliberately places the greatest emphasis on **Business Value**.

The framework is designed to answer:

> **Which opportunities have the strongest combination of business impact and practical ability to deliver that impact?**

---

# 3. Business Value — 40%

### Question

**How significant could the potential business outcome be?**

Consider:

* Revenue opportunity
* Cost reduction
* Productivity
* Customer experience
* Employee experience
* Risk reduction
* Strategic capability
* Competitive advantage

### Scoring

**1 = Very Low**

Minimal benefit with limited business impact.

**2 = Low**

Modest benefit affecting a localized process, team or activity.

**3 = Moderate**

Meaningful improvement to a business process or function.

**4 = High**

Significant financial, customer, productivity, operational or risk impact.

**5 = Very High**

Transformational enterprise-level outcome, major financial impact or significant competitive advantage.

---

# 4. Strategic Alignment — 15%

### Question

**How strongly does the opportunity support strategic business priorities?**

Consider whether the opportunity:

* Supports a stated strategic priority
* Advances a transformation agenda
* Strengthens a critical organizational capability
* Supports competitive positioning
* Enables an important future-state operating model

### Scoring

**1 = Very Low**

Little or no connection to strategic priorities.

**2 = Low**

Weak or indirect alignment.

**3 = Moderate**

Supports an established strategic priority.

**4 = High**

Directly advances a major strategic objective.

**5 = Very High**

Critical to achieving a strategic transformation or competitive position.

---

# 5. Feasibility — 15%

### Question

**How achievable is the opportunity with the organization's current or realistically obtainable capabilities?**

Consider:

* Data availability
* Data quality
* Technology readiness
* Process maturity
* Integration requirements
* Skills and capabilities
* Operating model
* Change requirements
* Implementation complexity
* Organizational readiness

### Scoring

**1 = Very Difficult**

Major barriers exist and significant new capabilities may be required.

**2 = Difficult**

Significant technical, data or organizational challenges exist.

**3 = Moderate**

Achievable with moderate investment, capability development and organizational change.

**4 = High**

High feasibility with most required capabilities available.

**5 = Very High**

Straightforward to implement with capabilities largely available.

---

# 6. Time to Value — 15%

### Question

**How quickly can measurable business value realistically be achieved?**

| Score | Indicative Time to Value |
| ----: | ------------------------ |
| **1** | More than 24 months      |
| **2** | 12–24 months             |
| **3** | 6–12 months              |
| **4** | 3–6 months               |
| **5** | Less than 3 months       |

These ranges are indicative and can be adapted to organizational or industry circumstances.

---

# 7. Risk Manageability — 15%

### Question

**How manageable are the risks associated with implementing and operating the AI capability?**

Consider:

* Data privacy
* Cybersecurity
* Accuracy
* Bias
* Regulatory requirements
* Intellectual property
* Model reliability
* Human oversight
* Reputational impact
* Operational resilience

### Scoring

**1 = Very Difficult to Manage**

Significant exposure with limited practical mitigation.

**2 = Difficult to Manage**

High risk requiring substantial mitigation and governance.

**3 = Manageable**

Moderate risk with credible controls available.

**4 = Easy to Manage**

Low risk with established controls and governance.

**5 = Very Easy to Manage**

Very low risk with strong controls and limited exposure.

A higher score represents a **more manageable risk profile**.

---

# 8. Opportunity Attractiveness Score

The five dimensions produce an **Opportunity Attractiveness Score**.

The calculation is:

> **Opportunity Attractiveness = (Business Value × 40%) + (Strategic Alignment × 15%) + (Feasibility × 15%) + (Time to Value × 15%) + (Risk Manageability × 15%)**

The score is normalized to a **100-point scale**.

### Example

An opportunity scoring:

* Business Value = 5
* Strategic Alignment = 4
* Feasibility = 4
* Time to Value = 4
* Risk Manageability = 4

would produce:

**(5 × 40%) + (4 × 15%) + (4 × 15%) + (4 × 15%) + (4 × 15%)**

= **4.4 / 5**

= **88 / 100**

The score indicates opportunity attractiveness before detailed financial analysis.

It does **not** by itself determine whether the organization should invest.

---

# 9. Evidence and Validation

Probability of success is **not treated as a separate weighted scoring dimension**.

Instead, confidence should be established through evidence.

Leadership should ask:

* Has the opportunity been tested?
* Is there evidence that the proposed AI capability works?
* Are the underlying data assumptions validated?
* Are users likely to adopt the solution?
* Has value been demonstrated elsewhere?
* Are the expected benefits measurable?
* Are the key implementation assumptions understood?
* What uncertainties remain?

Where uncertainty is significant, the appropriate response may be to **experiment or investigate** before making a larger investment.

This prevents an apparently attractive score from creating false confidence.

---

# 10. Portfolio Prioritization

Individual opportunity scores should be considered alongside the wider portfolio.

Leadership should consider:

* Relative attractiveness
* Strategic balance
* Available investment capacity
* Organizational capacity
* Dependencies
* Risk concentration
* Timing
* Existing transformation commitments
* Potential duplication between initiatives

An opportunity with a high individual score may not be the next investment if the portfolio already contains similar initiatives or if required resources are constrained.

Portfolio prioritization therefore answers:

> **"Given our available resources and competing opportunities, where should we focus?"**

---

# 11. Decision Categories

Use the Opportunity Attractiveness Score as an important input into four broad decision categories.

### Prioritize

The opportunity demonstrates strong overall attractiveness and should receive leadership attention.

**Recommended action:**

Progress toward detailed business case development, discovery or pilot planning.

---

### Experiment

The opportunity has meaningful potential, but evidence is insufficient to justify a larger commitment.

**Recommended action:**

Run a controlled experiment or pilot designed to validate key assumptions.

---

### Investigate

The opportunity may be strategically or commercially important, but material uncertainty remains.

Examples include:

* Data uncertainty
* Technical feasibility
* Economic uncertainty
* Risk or governance concerns
* Organizational readiness

**Recommended action:**

Resolve the most important uncertainties before committing significant resources.

---

### De-prioritize

The opportunity does not currently justify priority relative to competing opportunities.

Potential reasons include:

* Limited business value
* Weak strategic alignment
* Low feasibility
* Slow time to value
* Difficult-to-manage risk
* Better alternatives elsewhere in the portfolio

**Recommended action:**

Do not allocate significant resources at this stage.

---

# 12. Prioritization Is Not Investment Approval

A high Opportunity Attractiveness Score does not automatically mean:

> **Invest.**

The next step is to establish the economic case.

The broader decision model is:

**Opportunity Attractiveness + Portfolio Position + Economics + Evidence → Investment Decision**

The future **AI Business Case Framework** should assess:

* Initial investment
* Implementation cost
* Ongoing operating cost
* Expected benefits
* Revenue impact
* Cost reduction
* Productivity benefit
* ROI
* Payback period
* Expected value

---

# 13. Executive Decision Questions

Before moving a high-priority opportunity toward investment, leadership should ask:

1. What business problem are we solving?
2. What measurable outcome are we targeting?
3. Why is AI the appropriate solution?
4. What alternatives have been considered?
5. What is the expected business value?
6. What evidence supports the expected outcome?
7. How feasible is implementation?
8. What are the major risks?
9. How quickly can value be realized?
10. What investment is required?
11. What would cause us to stop or change direction?
12. What evidence would justify scaling?

---

# 14. Guiding Principles

### Outcome over technology

Start with the business outcome rather than the AI technology.

### Value over volume

A smaller portfolio of high-value opportunities is preferable to a large portfolio of low-value experiments.

### Portfolio over individual opportunities

Resources should be allocated across the portfolio rather than solely according to individual scores.

### Economics matter

Potential value must be considered against the investment required to achieve it.

### Evidence before scale

Where uncertainty is material, use experimentation to generate evidence before committing significant resources.

### Value realization is the ultimate measure

An AI initiative is successful when it delivers measurable business outcomes, not simply when the technology is implemented.

---

# 15. Decision Journey

The complete AI investment journey is:

**1. Business Problem**

Identify the business problem and desired outcome.

↓

**2. Opportunity Assessment**

Determine whether AI could create meaningful, practical and responsible value.

↓

**3. Portfolio Prioritization**

Compare opportunities and focus resources on the strongest candidates.

↓

**4. Business Case**

Assess investment, benefits, ROI and payback.

↓

**5. Investment Decision**

Determine whether to invest, experiment, investigate or de-prioritize.

↓

**6. Execution**

Turn the approved opportunity into a managed initiative.

↓

**7. Value Realization**

Measure actual outcomes against the original investment case.

---

# Conclusion

AI portfolios should be managed as **business investments rather than collections of technology experiments**.

The objective is to create a disciplined process for moving from:

**Business Problem → Opportunity → Assessment → Prioritization → Evidence → Business Case → Investment → Execution → Value Realization**

The prioritization framework provides the bridge between identifying attractive AI opportunities and making disciplined investment decisions.

This framework is independently developed for professional portfolio purposes. All examples, scoring ranges and weightings are illustrative and should be adapted to organizational circumstances.
